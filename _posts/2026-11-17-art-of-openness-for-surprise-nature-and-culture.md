**What happens when scientific research produces something we did not expect? And perhaps more importantly, are we genuinely prepared to change our minds?**

Research proposals are full of promises: innovation, impact, measurable outcomes, and solutions that can be scaled up. We are expected to define our objectives, anticipate results, and demonstrate how our research will contribute to solving societal challenges.

But there is something paradoxical about this approach. **How can we discover something genuinely new if we already know what we are supposed to find?**

This question is at the heart of our newly published paper, *"The Art of Openness for Surprise: Observing Knowledge Production at Work,"* in the journal *Nature and Culture* (2026).

## Turning the analytical gaze on ourselves

Drawing on Science and Technology Studies (STS), particularly Sheila Jasanoff's idiom of co-production and Matthias Gross's sociology of ignorance and surprise, we decided to do something that researchers perhaps do not do often enough: **critically examine our own knowledge production.**

Rather than studying scientific practices from a distance, we reflected on five research projects in which members of our team at the Institute of Sociology, Jagiellonian University, have been directly involved:

- **PANTHEON** – modelling decarbonisation pathways and integrating stakeholder perspectives into established scientific models.
- **LIGHT** – investigating health literacy and the assumptions underlying health education in Polish schools.
- **VAX-TRUST** – exploring vaccine hesitancy and questioning how scientific and institutional actors define the problem.
- **FAMOS** – co-designing offshore floating platforms and asking whether technologically possible futures are necessarily socially desirable.
- **Needs RePowered (Plug&Chill)** – experimenting with decentralised energy sharing while acknowledging that the proposed business model might ultimately prove unworkable.

Through three reflexive workshops and comparisons across these projects, we explored how institutional expectations, disciplinary traditions, methodological choices, and even our own emotions shape the knowledge we produce.

## Three ways of being open to surprise

Our analysis identifies three interconnected modes of openness.

**First, awareness of limitations.** Sometimes the most important discovery is recognising what our existing theories, methods, or models cannot explain. In PANTHEON, for instance, stakeholder perspectives reveal aspects of decarbonisation futures that are difficult to translate into the established language of integrated assessment models.

**Second, rethinking initial assumptions.** Genuine openness means accepting that our original research questions might themselves need revision. VAX-TRUST illustrates how vaccine hesitancy, initially approached as a problem requiring intervention, can instead be understood as a socially meaningful expression of uncertainty, responsibility, and institutional trust.

**Third, readiness for failure.** What if a solution simply does not work? In Plug&Chill, the potential non-viability of a decentralised energy-sharing model is not necessarily a scientific failure. It may reveal important structural, economic, and regulatory barriers that would otherwise remain invisible.

These modes challenge the idea that successful research is necessarily research that delivers what was originally promised.

## Why does this matter?

One of our main arguments is that **openness is not simply an individual virtue or a methodological declaration. It is something that must be made possible by the very organisation of research.**

We introduce the concept of the *co-productive setting* to describe how funding requirements, consortium cultures, researchers' expectations, participants' perspectives, and affective dispositions collectively shape what can be known.

This also raises an uncomfortable question about participatory and interdisciplinary research. Inviting stakeholders into a project does not automatically make knowledge production more open. Their contributions might challenge established frameworks, but they can also be filtered, translated, or accommodated in ways that leave underlying assumptions largely unchanged.

And there is another dimension we rarely discuss openly: emotions. Pride, curiosity, enthusiasm, anxiety, disappointment, or fear of failure are not external to scientific practice. They influence whether we welcome unexpected findings or quietly try to protect our initial assumptions.

## Why is this paper worth reading?

We believe the article speaks to a broader debate about how contemporary science is organised, funded, and evaluated.

It offers a conceptual framework for examining epistemic openness, but also a reflexive account of the tensions we encounter when working across disciplines, institutions, and societal expectations.

For researchers working in STS, sociology of science, interdisciplinary research, participatory approaches, or science policy, the paper invites reflection on a fundamental distinction: **Are we producing new knowledge, or becoming increasingly efficient at delivering knowledge that was already anticipated?**

We do not argue that research should abandon planning, methodological rigour, or accountability. Rather, we suggest that scientific quality also depends on our ability to recognise ignorance, reconsider assumptions, and learn from outcomes that do not fit the original plan.

Ultimately, the article is an invitation to think differently about what scientific success means.

**Perhaps the most important question is not whether our research projects achieve their predefined objectives, but whether they remain open enough to discover something we never expected.**
